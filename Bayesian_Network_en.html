1. Fundamentals of Bayesian Networks (BN)

A Bayesian Network (BN) is a Probabilistic Graphical Model (PGM) that visualizes causal relationships and statistical dependencies among uncertain variables as a directed graph, and uses that structure to perform conditional probability inference.

1.1 Motivation: Simplifying High-Dimensional Joint Distributions

Suppose we want to forecast river inflow by simultaneously accounting for 10 variables, such as rainfall, soil moisture, temperature, and upstream water levels.

  • Limitation of direct multivariate joint distributions: Estimating the full joint probability distribution $P(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n)$ monolithically causes the number of parameters and data requirements to grow exponentially as variables increase (the curse of dimensionality).
  • The Bayesian Network solution (exploiting conditional independence): BNs leverage the physical fact that "not every variable directly influences every other variable." For instance, once the downstream gauge directly upstream of the dam is accurately observed, information from a far-upstream gauge hundreds of kilometers away adds no direct influence on tomorrow's inflow given the downstream observation.
  • By drawing arcs only between directly interacting variables and factoring out indirect paths via conditional independence, a complex high-dimensional joint distribution decomposes into a compact product of local conditional distributions.

1.2 Three Core Components of a Bayesian Network

  1. Nodes: Random variables being modeled (e.g., rainfall, soil moisture, upstream water level, dam inflow).
  2. Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG): A set of directed arcs (edges) encoding direct causal or statistical influence.
    • When an arc points $X_j \to X_i$, $X_j$ is called a parent node and $X_i$ a child node.
    • Directed cycles returning to the starting node are strictly prohibited to preserve mathematical consistency.
  3. Quantitative Conditional Specification: The conditional probability distribution of each child given its parents. Discrete BNs use Conditional Probability Tables (CPTs), whereas continuous networks such as the one in this thesis use empirical marginals coupled with conditional copulas.
$$P(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P\big(X_i \mid \text{pa}(X_i)\big)$$

(Here, $\text{pa}(X_i)$ denotes the set of direct parent nodes of $X_i$.)

1.3 Intuitive Example and Hydrological Hierarchy

Whereas multiple linear regression or deep neural networks (DNNs) feed all 10 input variables directly into a single output node (a 10-to-1 fan-in structure), a Bayesian network mirrors the physical river routing (upstream $\to$ midstream $\to$ downstream) like stepping stones, keeping the number of parents per node minimal.

1.4 Bidirectional Inference and Key Advantages

1) Predictive Reasoning (Forward)

Cause $\to$ Effect: "Given an 80% rainfall forecast today, what is the probability that the grass will be wet (or what is the distribution of tomorrow's dam inflow)?" Inference follows the arc directions to compute future state distributions.

2) Diagnostic Reasoning (Backward)

Effect $\to$ Cause: "Looking outside in the morning, the grass is wet (observed evidence). What are the posterior probabilities that it rained overnight versus that the sprinkler ran?" Bayes' rule propagates backward against the arcs to update causal probabilities.

  • Robustness to Missing Data: If an operational sensor (such as an upstream water-level gauge) fails and drops out, the BN automatically infers the conditional distribution of the missing node from connected neighbors and continues forecasting reliably.
  • High Explainability: Unlike black-box models, the graph structure and rank correlations make it transparent which hydrological drivers affect the forecast and through which causal paths.

2. How Copulas Are Applied in Bayesian Networks (NPBN)

To enable the Bayesian network described in Section 1 to handle continuous, strongly skewed hydrological variables—such as rainfall ($\text{mm}$), soil moisture, and river discharge ($\text{m}^3/\text{s}$)—without distorting their distributions, a Non-Parametric Bayesian Network (NPBN) is used. An NPBN directly integrates copula theory into the nodes and arcs of the DAG.

2.2 Coupling DAG Components with Copulas (Quantifying Nodes & Arcs)

In a copula-based NPBN, the two structural elements of a Bayesian network—nodes and arcs—are quantified separately as follows:

  • Nodes $\leftrightarrow$ Marginal Distributions ($F_i$): Rather than imposing a parametric family (such as Gaussian) on node $X_i$, each node retains its Empirical Cumulative Distribution Function (Empirical CDF, $F_i$) estimated directly from historical observations.
  • Arcs ($X_j \to X_i$) $\leftrightarrow$ (Conditional) Rank Correlations ($r$) & Copula Parameters ($\rho$):
    • When a child node $X_i$ has only a single parent $X_j$, the arc is assigned the unconditional Spearman rank correlation $r(X_j, X_i)$.
    • When a child node $X_i$ has multiple parents ($X_{p_1}, X_{p_2}, \dots, X_{p_k}$), the parents are ordered and each arc is assigned a conditional rank correlation $r(X_{p_m}, X_i \mid X_{p_1}, \dots, X_{p_{m-1}})$ controlling for preceding parents. These conditional rank correlations can be specified independently for every arc without violating algebraic consistency (positive definiteness).
    • Every assigned rank correlation $r$ is transformed via the Gaussian copula identity $\rho = 2\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{6}r\right)$ into a (partial) correlation in latent normal space. Combined with the conditional independencies encoded by missing arcs in the DAG, this uniquely specifies the network-wide latent correlation matrix $\Sigma$.

2.3 Multivariate Conditional Inference Procedure in a Copula BN

Once the network-wide latent correlation matrix $\Sigma$ is constructed, the 4-step bivariate Gaussian copula pipeline generalizes seamlessly to any number of nodes ($n$). Let $E$ denote the set of observed evidence nodes and $T$ denote the unobserved target nodes to predict or diagnose. Inference proceeds in three analytical steps:

STEP 1 Project Observed Evidence Nodes to Latent Normal Space ($x_E \to u_E \to z_E$)

Pass each currently observed variable (e.g., today's upstream gauge level, cumulative rainfall, soil moisture) through its empirical CDF $F_E$ and inverse standard normal CDF $\Phi^{-1}$ to form the latent normal vector $z_E = \Phi^{-1}(F_E(x_E))$. If a sensor drops out, simply omit that variable from $E$ and condition on the remaining observations.

STEP 2 Multivariate Conditional Update in Latent Normal Space ($Z_T \mid Z_E = z_E$)

Partitioning the full latent correlation matrix $\Sigma$ into target ($T$) and evidence ($E$) blocks yields the exact conditional latent distribution in closed form via a single matrix operation—with no numerical integration or MCMC required:

$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \Sigma_{TT} & \Sigma_{TE} \\ \Sigma_{ET} & \Sigma_{EE} \end{pmatrix} \quad \implies \quad Z_T \mid (Z_E = z_E) \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\Sigma_{TE}\Sigma_{EE}^{-1}z_E, \; \Sigma_{TT} - \Sigma_{TE}\Sigma_{EE}^{-1}\Sigma_{ET}\right)$$

This single equation uniformly handles forward prediction (cause $\to$ effect), backward diagnosis (effect $\to$ cause), and missing-sensor imputation.

STEP 3 Return to Percentiles and Invert to Physical Units ($Z_T \to U_T \to X_T$)

Transform forecast draws (or Monte Carlo ensemble samples) $Z_T$ from Step 2 through the standard normal CDF $\Phi(\cdot)$ back into percentiles $U_T = \Phi(Z_T)$, then apply each target node's empirical inverse CDF $F_T^{-1}$ to recover the skewed, physical-unit conditional forecast distribution $X_T = F_T^{-1}(U_T)$.

Practical Caveat: Validating the Gaussian Copula and Arc Pruning In exchange for fast, closed-form conditional updates, an NPBN approximates all joint dependencies using a Gaussian copula ($\lambda_U = \lambda_L = 0$; see Section 2 of the Copula Summary) and prunes weak arcs via conditional independence. Consequently, one must quantitatively verify that the sparse Gaussian-copula network preserves empirical dependence—motivating the $d$-calibration validation framework in Section 4.

3. Extension to Dynamic Bayesian Networks (DBN & 2TBN Structure)

A Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) extends the static Bayesian network from Section 1 by explicitly modeling transitions across time ($t \to t+1$). Here, "dynamic" does not mean the graph topology changes at every moment; rather, it means the model dynamically tracks how system states evolve across successive time slices.

3.1 Two-Time-Slice Bayesian Network (2TBN) Architecture

Unfolding a 10-day forecast horizon for 12 variables ($12 \times 10 = 120$ nodes) into a single static BN would trigger the curse of dimensionality and make correlation matrix estimation intractable. Instead, a 2TBN defines only two consecutive time slices ($t$ and $t+1$, totaling 24 nodes):

  • Intra-slice arcs: Spatial and physical causal dependencies within the same time step (e.g., $P_t \to S_t \to Q_t$).
  • Inter-slice arcs: Temporal persistence and lag effects carrying across from time $t$ to time $t+1$ (e.g., $Q_t \to Q_{t+1}$).
  • First-order Markov property & time homogeneity: The state at $t+1$ depends solely on the immediately preceding slice $t$, and the $t \to t+1$ transition structure remains stationary over time.
  • Unrolling: Copying and chaining the 2TBN template $H$ times along the time axis sequentially generates multi-step future forecast trajectories over 10–15 days (rollout).

3.2 Hybrid Construction of Conditional Independence (Arc Selection)

  1. Stage 1 (Domain Knowledge): Establish the physical backbone following river flow from upstream to downstream (upstream Sourou $\to$ midstream Lawra $\to$ downstream Chache).
  2. Stage 2 (Data-Driven Expansion): Inspect rank correlations and structural similarity scores to incrementally add strongly correlated arcs.
  3. Stage 3 (Physical Plausibility Filtering): Reject spurious correlations that cannot be justified by spatial proximity or hydrological causality, yielding a parsimonious DAG.

4. Bui Dam Case Study & Validation Framework

Using the 2019 emergency spillway release event at the Bui Dam in Ghana's Black Volta basin as a case study, DBN-based probabilistic inflow forecasting was coupled with CVaR-based Stochastic Model Predictive Control (SMPC).

4.1 Direct DBN vs. Residual DBN and Basin Lag Identification

  • Hydrological lag identification: For each explanatory variable $X$ and target inflow $Y$, the physical travel time lag $L$ was selected as the shift maximizing the absolute Spearman rank correlation $|\rho_s(X_{t-L}, Y_t)|$.
  • Direct DBN: A surrogate model predicting Chache inflow directly from meteorological and hydrological drivers ($P_{7D}$, antecedent precipitation index API, soil moisture, upstream flows). It is computationally fast and captures rising flood limbs well, though a single fixed lag and first-order Markov assumption tend to underestimate peak magnitudes and lag during flood recession.
  • Residual DBN: A post-processing model trained on log-multiplicative residuals $\eta_t = \ln(Q_{obs,t} / Q_{mod,t})$ of an existing deterministic hydrological model. Strong autocorrelation (persistence) in the residuals causes initial errors to carry forward over the horizon.

4.2 Theoretical Basis of the $d$-calibration Score & Three Correlation Matrices

In a 24-variable high-dimensional DBN, pairwise scatter plots alone cannot assess global model fit. Therefore, the thesis uses the $d$-calibration score, which measures information-geometric similarity across the entire correlation matrix.

(1) Mathematical Derivation and Similarity Properties of $d$-calibration

The overlap between two zero-mean multivariate normal distributions $\mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_1)$ and $\mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma_2)$ is measured by the Bhattacharyya coefficient ($\eta$):

$$\eta = \frac{\det(\Sigma_1)^{1/4}\det(\Sigma_2)^{1/4}}{\det\left[\frac{1}{2}(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2)\right]^{1/2}}$$

The Hellinger distance ($H$) between the two distributions is defined as $H(\Sigma_1, \Sigma_2) = \sqrt{1 - \eta}$, and subtracting it from $1$ gives the $d$-calibration score:

$$d_{cal}(\Sigma_1, \Sigma_2) = 1 - H(\Sigma_1, \Sigma_2) = 1 - \sqrt{1 - \eta}$$
  • Behavior at extremes ($0 \lt d_{cal} \le 1$): When two correlation matrices match identically ($\Sigma_1 = \Sigma_2$), the determinant ratio $\eta = 1$, giving $H = 0$ and $d_{cal} = 1$. Conversely, as two correlation structures diverge completely, $\eta \to 0$ and $d_{cal} \to 0$. Thus, $d_{cal}$ is a similarity metric, not a distance.
  • Advantage over element-wise metrics (e.g., RMSE): Correlation matrices must remain positive semi-definite and reside on a Riemannian covariance manifold. Unlike element-wise averaging, Hellinger-based $d_{cal}$ jointly captures differences in overall multivariate volume and directional structure.

(2) Exact Meaning of the Three Rank-Correlation Matrices Used for Validation

To separately test the validity of the Gaussian copula assumption and the information lost by pruning arcs in the DAG, three rank-correlation matrices are constructed and compared:

1) $R_{ERC}$ — Empirical Rank-Correlation Matrix

  • Computation: Directly compute Spearman's rank correlation $\rho_s(X_i, X_j)$ across all variable pairs $(X_i, X_j)$ from observed (raw or deseasonalised) time series without any distributional assumptions.
  • Meaning (Ground Truth): Represents the unfiltered multivariate dependence structure actually present in the hydrological data, free of Gaussian copula or graph assumptions.

2) $R_{NRC}$ — Normal Rank-Correlation Matrix

  • Computation (3-step transformation):
    1. Map each observed variable through its empirical CDF and inverse standard normal CDF into latent normal scores ($Z_i = \Phi^{-1}(F_i(X_i))$).
    2. Compute the standard Pearson linear correlation matrix $\Sigma = [\rho_{ij}]$ across all pairs in normal-score space.
    3. Convert each $\rho_{ij}$ back into rank correlation via the Gaussian copula identity $r_{ij} = \frac{6}{\pi}\arcsin(\rho_{ij}/2)$.
  • Meaning (Saturated Gaussian Copula Benchmark): Represents the theoretical rank-correlation matrix that would appear if the data followed a fully connected (saturated) multivariate Gaussian copula.

3) $R_{BNRC}$ — Bayesian Network-Implied Rank-Correlation Matrix

  • Computation: In the parsimonious DBN where unnecessary arcs have been pruned, propagate the conditional rank correlations assigned to the remaining arcs through the DAG paths to reconstruct the implied rank correlation for every variable pair.
  • Meaning (Dependence Reproduced by the Sparse DBN): Pairs lacking direct arcs (assumed conditionally independent) acquire correlation solely through indirect paths, showing the exact dependence structure achievable by the simplified DBN model.

4.3 Deseasonalisation Effect, Rollout Modes & CVaR-Based SMPC Integration

  • Impact of Deseasonalisation: During the wet season (July–October), raw data exhibit inflated correlations driven by the shared seasonal cycle. The anomaly model (Rain-Anom), constructed by subtracting daily climatological means, improved the out-of-sample Energy Skill Score (ESS) by roughly 15% over the climatology ensemble and achieved the strongest generalization.
  • Rollout Comparison: Monte Carlo rollout samples random draws at every step, preserving upper-tail uncertainty at the cost of accumulating sampling variance; Conditional-mean rollout propagates conditional means ($K = 200$ average), yielding smooth trajectories with narrower extreme-tail spread.
  • CVaR-Based SMPC Optimization: The 500 inflow ensemble members generated by the DBN were reduced via k-means clustering into 100 representative scenarios ($\pi_s$), and soft CVaR constraints on the threshold reservoir level ($183\,\text{m}$) across risk-aversion levels $\beta \in \{0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 0.99\}$ were solved via Linear Programming (LP). Compared with actual manual operation in 2019, proactive pre-release through hydropower turbines reduced unproductive spillway releases by 8–77% and lowered peak reservoir levels by 0.1–1.0 m.
